Why should I care?

The president has limited, specific power to set tariffs, as determined by Congress. He then used an emergency law (the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, known as IEEPA) meant for sanctions, not tariffs, allowing him to skip oversight. The Supreme Court ruled them illegal. Trump continues to impose tariffs under different justifications. Connected companies get exemptions; you do not.

Between February 2025 and January 2026, Trump's tariffs cost each household an average of $1,745.

"IEEPA authorizes the President to 'investigate, block…regulate, direct and compel, nullify, void, prevent or prohibit…importation or exportation.' Absent from this lengthy list of powers is any mention of tariffs or duties."

— Chief Justice John Roberts, majority opinion, Learning Resources v. Trump, February 20, 2026

What's IEEPA? The International Emergency Economic Powers Act lets the president freeze assets, block transactions, and restrict trade with a specific foreign adversary during a declared national emergency — think sanctions on a hostile government. It has never once been used to impose tariffs, and the law's text doesn't mention tariffs or duties at all. The Supreme Court agreed: that's simply not what the law is for.

Legal whack-a-mole

After the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, the administration didn't stop — it just switched to other legal authorities, some legitimate and long-standing, some brand new and untested.

The pharmaceutical exemptions are an explicit trade: sign a drug-pricing deal with the administration, get up to 3 years of tariff protection in return. Beyond these two confirmed categories, we don't know the full list — because the process itself isn't public.
