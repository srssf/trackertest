Cost of tariffs — updated

Today Trump's tariffs cost the average household an average of

$3.01

For a total of $1,100 this year under the tariffs currently in effect — down from earlier estimates because the Supreme Court struck down the broadest tariffs in February, but not eliminated.

From the source

"Estimated household costs due to tariffs total about $1,100 annually under current law," with the average statutory tariff rate now at 11.0% (projected to reach 11.8% by year's end) and an estimated 0.7% overall bump to consumer prices from tariff policy.

— The Budget Lab at Yale, "The State of U.S. Tariffs" (updated August 24, 2026)

What changed

"IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs," the Supreme Court held in a 6–3 ruling on February 20, 2026, striking down the "reciprocal" tariffs on most trading partners and the fentanyl- and immigration-related tariffs on China, Canada, and Mexico — the two programs that had produced most of the daily cost figures this page previously cited. (See the Criminal Activity page for the ruling itself.)

— Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, 607 U.S. ___ (2026), SCOTUSblog case file

What's still in effect

Tariffs imposed under Section 232 (steel, aluminum, copper, autos, pharmaceuticals, lumber, furniture) and Section 301 (forced-labor tariffs on roughly 60 trading partners, Brazil) don't rely on the emergency-powers law the Court struck down, and both are described as "durable" with "low litigation risk." A separate 10% global tariff invoked under Section 122 as a stopgap was itself ruled unlawful by the Court of International Trade in May 2026 and expired under its own 150-day statutory limit on July 24, 2026. A newly invoked, century-old authority (Section 338) is now being used for a 50% tariff on Canada as of August 2026, but has never been tested in court.

— Thomson Reuters, "After IEEPA, which tariff authorities does the U.S. government still have?"

The refunds

"While importers will experience some relief by receiving refunds, the economic damage from the chaotic tariff regime cannot be refunded — and the remaining tariffs means economic damage will continue to grow." The government owes an estimated $166–179 billion in duties collected under the tariffs later ruled illegal. It paid out roughly $100 billion of that by August 2026 — including $49.18 billion in June alone, against just $23.63 billion in new collections that month, a net loss of $25.56 billion for the U.S. Treasury in a single month.

— Tax Foundation, via Fortune, "The $100 billion in refunds have wiped out revenue the import taxes brought in since May" (August 21, 2026)

  • Joint Economic Committee (Democrats): family tariff-cost estimate

    This March 2026 estimate of $2,500 per family assumed the tariffs later struck down would stay in place all year — it's out of date now, but included here since it was this page's original household-cost source.

Methodology. This page's headline figure comes from the Budget Lab at Yale, which models household tariff costs under whatever tariffs are legally in effect right now — as of its August 24, 2026 update, that's about $1,100 per household for the year, which this page divides by 365 for a daily average. That's a smaller number than the roughly $2,500 per-family estimate this page cited earlier in 2026, because it no longer includes the "reciprocal" and fentanyl/immigration tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February (see "What changed" above) — but it isn't zero, because Section 232 and 301 tariffs, plus a new Section 338 tariff on Canada, remain in effect and still raise prices. Because several of those remaining authorities are still being challenged in court, this number could change again; the Budget Lab's tracker updates roughly every few weeks as the legal picture shifts.
