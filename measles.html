Why should I care?

Measles was eliminated in the United States in 2000. The US is now at risk of losing that status.

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases on earth, but it's also one of the most preventable — two doses of the MMR vaccine are about 97% effective. Keeping that status requires roughly 95% of the population to be vaccinated. National kindergarten vaccination rates have since fallen to 92.5%, and only 10 states plus Washington, D.C. still meet that threshold. The Health and Human Services Department has removed $11.4 billion in public-health funding, forcing health departments nationwide to cancel vaccine clinics. In June 2025, Secretary Robert F. Kennedy fired all 17 members of the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices. The current measles outbreak has grown into the worst the U.S. has seen since 1991, and international health officials are reviewing this November whether the country has lost the elimination status it held for 26 years.

"In 2026, decades after measles was eliminated in the United States, it is heartbreaking to witness deaths from a disease that is preventable with a vaccine that has been widely used since the 1970s."

— Dr. Andrew Racine, President, American Academy of Pediatrics, August 2026

What's "measles elimination status," and why is it at risk? The U.S. declared measles eliminated in 2000, meaning the virus was no longer spreading continuously here — every case was either imported from abroad or a short, contained chain of transmission. The U.S. loses that status if the same strain spreads continuously, uninterrupted, for 12 straight months. The current outbreak has been spreading since early 2025; the Pan American Health Organization is reviewing the case this November to decide whether the U.S. has, in fact, lost the elimination status it held for a quarter century.

What's the ACIP, and why does it matter that all 17 members were fired? The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices is an independent panel of medical and scientific experts that reviews vaccine safety and effectiveness data and recommends the vaccine schedule that pediatricians, insurers, and the federal Vaccines for Children program all follow. Kennedy called the sitting committee "a rubber stamp" and dismissed all 17 members in a single day, promising replacements who would not be "ideological anti-vaxxers." Medical organizations warned the move politicized a process designed specifically to stay insulated from politics.
