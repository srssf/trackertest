Why should I care?

The Trump administration says this is about deporting criminals. The data says otherwise: 73% of people in detention have no criminal conviction — only 5% have a violent one. Courts have repeatedly found the administration is breaking the law to make that happen: as of August 2026, eight federal appeals courts — most recently the Third Circuit — have ruled that ICE can't lock people up for months or years with no hearing at all. Detained children are supposed to be protected by a binding 1997 court settlement requiring humane treatment and family reunification; the administration tried to end that settlement outright, and a federal judge refused, ruling the government hadn't even met its existing obligations. And 2025 was the deadliest stretch in ICE custody in almost two decades — worse than 2020, when COVID-19 swept through detention facilities.

"Because their detentions without hearings violated the Immigration and Nationality Act and due process, we will affirm."

— Judge Patty Shwartz, U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, ruling ICE's mandatory-detention policy unlawful, August 28, 2026

What's a "bond hearing," and why does this ruling matter? Normally, someone ICE detains can ask a judge for a bond hearing — a chance to argue they should be released while their immigration case plays out. In 2025 the administration adopted a policy denying that hearing altogether to certain immigrants, meaning they could be held for months or years with no chance to make their case. This is now the eighth federal appeals court to say that policy is illegal — a violation of both immigration law and the Constitution's due-process protections. The administration is expected to keep appealing, possibly to the Supreme Court.

Methodology. The booking average combines the most recently published ICE interior enforcement and CBP daily rates; none of these sources currently publish a literal same-day count, so figures here are the latest available rather than real-time. Death counts are shown as two separate lines — total deaths in custody (Human Rights Watch) and total killed by agents outside custody (a separately sourced running tally) — rather than a single stacked figure, since the two are documented differently and shouldn't be added together without care.
